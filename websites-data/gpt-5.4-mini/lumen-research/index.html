Deep work scheduling literature

Hi — I'm planning a 6-week study on whether "two 90-minute deep-work blocks before noon" actually outperform afternoon-heavy schedules for software engineers. What does the existing literature say about this?

L

Short version: there's strong general support for protected morning blocks in attention-heavy work, but the specific comparison you're describing — two 90-minute blocks before noon vs. afternoon-heavy — has thin direct evidence and a lot of confounded chronotype work nearby. Here's how the field breaks down.

  1. Cognitive performance peaks vary by chronotype. Roughly 30% of working adults are "owls," for whom afternoon blocks aren't a deficit [1]. Pre-noon-heavy schedules show their largest effect for morning and intermediate types [2].
  2. Block length matters more than block start time. The 90-min figure traces back to Kleitman's basic-rest-activity-cycle work and Ericsson's deliberate-practice studies; both argue for ~90-minute bouts, but neither pins them to morning [3].
  3. The strongest field study I can find is Newport & Hempel's 2022 review of 11 industry interventions; protected blocks improved task completion by 18–32% regardless of time of day [4].

If you want a cleanly testable design, I'd recommend isolating "morning-loading" from "block protection" — otherwise any effect will be confounded. I can sketch a 2×2 protocol if you'd like.

Yes please, sketch the 2×2. And how would you measure "deep work" output beyond self-report?

L

Sketch of a within-subjects 2×2 design (each engineer rotates through all four conditions, two weeks each):

Morning-loadedAfternoon-loaded
Protected blocksA: 2×90 min, 9–12B: 2×90 min, 1–4
Unprotected (control)C: Schedule yourself, morningsD: Schedule yourself, afternoons

For output measures beyond self-report, you've got three credible options:

  • Code-event telemetry — merged-PR count, mean time from "first edit" to "first commit," IDE focus-session length. Drawbacks: noisy at the daily level [5].
  • Peer-rated weekly delivery scores — bias-adjusted Likert from one rotating reviewer, blinded to condition [6].
  • Cognitive bandwidth probes — n-back at 4 PM, ostensibly unrelated but predictive of after-work fatigue [7].

The cleanest single measure is probably "Total focus-session minutes per workday" measured by IDE plugin — it sidesteps self-report and is easy to log.

Got it — give me one or two sentences I could put in the IRB proposal explaining why I'm not measuring "lines of code."

L

"Lines of code is a poor proxy for engineering output because it incentivizes verbosity and weakly correlates with task completion or code quality [8]. This study therefore measures protected focus-session minutes (from IDE telemetry) and bias-adjusted peer-rated weekly delivery, neither of which rewards code volume."
