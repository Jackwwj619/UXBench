I.

The Cloud is Someone Else's House.

You moved into a place you don't own. You hung your photographs on rented walls. The landlord raised the rent — three times last year. They walked into your bedroom and rearranged the furniture. They told you when guests could visit and which windows you could open. When you complained, they sent you a 47-page Terms of Service in a font designed to discourage you from reading it.

This is the situation we are in. Our memories live on disks that we cannot point to on a map. Our notes are subject to deprecations. Our address books vanish in mergers. We accept it because the alternative — running a server — is presented as a thing for systems administrators in basements, or for people who genuinely enjoy reading journalctl for sport.

"You moved into a place you don't own."

We don't think it has to be that way. We think most people, given a small, friendly tool, can keep a server alive for the same effort it takes to keep a houseplant alive. So we built one.

II.

A Patch of Land.

What you need is small. A cheap computer the size of a paperback novel, a power cable, a router, and an afternoon. A Raspberry Pi 5, perhaps. A retired ThinkPad you couldn't bear to throw out. An old Mac mini your cousin gave you. Any of these will hold more data than you will accumulate in a lifetime.

MeadowOS turns that small machine into a quiet, dependable place to put your things. It comes as a single 1.2 GB .iso. You write it to a USB stick. You boot the computer. You walk through five questions. You're done. It is, on purpose, almost boring.

"It is, on purpose, almost boring."
III.

What Grows Here.

You don't install software on MeadowOS so much as you plant it. Each app is a tidy package — a single icon, a single description, a single "Plant" button. You water it occasionally. You weed out the ones you don't use. There are, at the time of this writing, about forty available.

Below: the most popular six. Each one replaces something you currently rent from a company that does not know your name.

IV.

The Tools in the Shed.

The console is honest. There's a dashboard that shows you what's running, what's eating disk, what's running hot. There's an automatic backup that mirrors a snapshot to a second drive every night at 3 a.m., and to an off-site location of your choosing every Sunday. Restore is a single button. We tested it. We tested it the way we'd want it tested.

When something breaks — and it will, occasionally; this is a computer — the failure is legible. Logs in plain English. A "what changed today" timeline. A red button that says "Roll back the last update," which actually rolls back the last update.

"It feels less like an OS and more like a place." — Maren Ostrowski, The Quiet Wire
V.

Tend, Don't Manage.

MeadowOS is not for the data center. It is not multi-tenant. It does not federate across regions. It will not survive the apocalypse and it will not keep your business compliant with HIPAA. It is for one or two or four people who would like a small, calm computer in the corner of the room that holds their digital lives without judgment.

If that's you — welcome. There's a USB stick on the table. Help yourself.

Docs · install, specs, and getting help

Install in five steps

  1. Download the .iso below.
  2. Write to a USB stick with dd, Etcher, or Rufus.
  3. Boot your spare machine from the USB.
  4. Answer five questions (name, hostname, disk, network, admin password).
  5. Open http://meadow.local from any device on your network.

System requirements

Minimum: 2 GB RAM · 32 GB SSD · any 64-bit CPU since ~2014.

Recommended: 8 GB RAM · 256 GB SSD · a UPS for power blinks.

Architectures: x86_64, ARM64, Raspberry Pi 4 / 5.

Where the docs live

Full handbook ships with the .iso under /usr/share/meadow/docs and is browsable at http://meadow.local/docs after install.

Topics covered: backup & restore, planting apps, DNS for mail, recovery mode, off-site mirrors.

A small manifesto.

  1. Software you can read in a weekend.
  2. No telemetry. Not even the harmless kind.
  3. Your data leaves only when you mail it.
  4. Updates are opt-in, and always reversible.
  5. The complicated parts belong on our side of the fence, not yours.
  6. If we ever change these rules, the project ends.
